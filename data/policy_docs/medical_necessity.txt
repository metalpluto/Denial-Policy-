Medical Necessity Determination Policy

A service is considered medically necessary when it is required to
diagnose or treat a patient's condition, consistent with generally
accepted standards of medical practice, and not primarily for the
convenience of the patient or provider.

Claims may be denied for lack of medical necessity when the submitted
diagnosis code does not clinically support the billed procedure, when a
less costly and equally effective alternative was available and not
attempted first, or when documentation does not establish that the
service was appropriate given the patient's presenting symptoms.

Common triggers for medical necessity denials include advanced imaging
(MRI, CT) ordered without a documented failed course of conservative
treatment, and procedures billed with a diagnosis code that reviewers
consider only loosely related to the service performed.

To appeal a medical necessity denial, the provider should submit clinical
notes establishing the presenting symptoms, prior treatments attempted,
and the clinical reasoning connecting the diagnosis to the necessity of
the specific procedure performed. A peer-to-peer review with the payer's
medical director may also be requested.

Medical necessity denials are distinct from coding errors: a medical
necessity denial means the payer disputes whether the service should
have been performed at all, not whether it was billed with the correct
code.
